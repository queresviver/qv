The Sun Never Began Because We Laughed First - Rencontres d'Arles 2026

by GM

The word kills, lives, and has always been the beginning. Precisely through its axe-like features, unhindered, it manages to kill so as to bring to life directives, concepts, personalities, emotions, waters, suns, metals and lives themselves. Photography is the art of those incapable of killing, incapable of seizing the substance of their creativity and giving it form, preferring an everlasting capture, licking jasmine pearls from the treasure of blood. Call it our ignorance; we call it the cowardice inherent in the enemies of craftsmen.

The image gives everything in the most perfect reconciliation of the immediate and of contempt for the dignity of the figure captured in the shell: the worst colour of all, the one that lies without saying why, the forms remaining the only witnesses to the error. It turns the Rhône into a pixelated panel of static, whose Judas hum carries us up to the highest clouds only to promptly burst the air bubble in which, like children, we had shut ourselves away.

Every hour, the cacophony of the river, of the infamous stumbling over the microclimates. Do they come here often? At least they rest in that slow love, three days of feasting in the necropolis of dreams. Of course these causes do not interest them: how the marvellous things of society, beautiful and serene, are organised. Prettier and funnier is the general fickleness of the shoes that slide like serpents.

If the image means to live, it has one chance: to become something bloody, to take facts, decant them and make them its own, as collage does, for example. Perhaps then it will finally be able to develop something human that is not mere veneration of the sun, that first image of all.

From 6 to 9 August 2026, QV had the chance to witness the 13th edition of Bons Sons, which marked the festival’s 20th anniversary, in the village of Cem Soldos, in Tomar.

Dreams We No Longer Remember - Bons Sons 2026

by MV

“The crickets, almost in perfect syncopation with the music heard in the distance, also take part in this nocturnal orchestra”

Preamble

A few days after Bons Sons, we look back on this edition of the festival with great fondness, and a yellow wave lights up those very well spent days. Returning to the village of Cem Soldos is always a pleasure, meeting again people we hold in great esteem, some who live there, others from neighbouring places, and still others who flock to the festival from more distant cities. Now that the festival has gone back to taking place every two years, any opportunity is a good one to revisit Tonita’s café, the Aliquete tavern and the Aleluia grills. Yet one of the things that struck us most this edition was also one of the spaces we liked least in years past: the campsite. Perhaps because of its very steep spots for pitching a tent, which luckily we escaped this year, never having had such a flat tent, or the long walk from the grounds to get there, or else the damp you feel on arriving in this area tucked into a small valley, camping had always been a troubled experience. This year, on the contrary, we felt it was a rich and challenging way of living the festival, forcing on us perhaps a more external perspective, one we are not so used to.

I

“Anyone got cinnamon? Ground cinnamon!” shouts a voice from a neighbouring tent. Shortly after, a group goes by with a mysterious dog whistle, causing laughter and fooling about. Tired, after pitching the tent, we stay in the comfort of the air mattress listening to Yakuza, who seem to be setting the stage in the village on fire and who reach the campsite more diffuse but still very vivid in the cool of the night. The crickets, almost in perfect syncopation with the music heard in the distance, also take part in this nocturnal orchestra. After a caldo verde and a toasted ham and cheese sandwich bought at the campsite bar, we didn’t have the courage to make the pilgrimage to the village, and we fell asleep listening to the distant party, a sleep later peopled with dreams we no longer remember.

II

Without sunglasses, the sun burns the eyes and reflects off the white of the stage. Near the pizza, with the smell of the wood-fired oven, we watch Família Gitana and the crowd dancing and shouting. Late into the night, between moshing and sweat, we shake ourselves loose to the rock of Victor Torpedo. A few hours earlier, in the main square of Cem Soldos, now renovated, we heard Luta Livre, whose political lyrics fill us with indignation mixed with energy and hope. As a curiosity, we later learnt that this project belongs to one of the members of Pesticida, later Despe e Siga. During the day, between dips in the incredibly warm reservoir and countless coffees, we revisited playlists and moments from earlier concerts, like when B Fachada, guest of the legendary Jorge Cruz (who, legend has it, withdrew from the stage because of tinnitus, a condition affecting the hearing, but returned like a meteoric phoenix to play in Cem Soldos), danced on stage, jumping with excitement like a child.

This concert, on one of our favourite Bons Sons stages, the old Olive Tree stage, so called for the two such trees in the middle of the stage, now named Palco Zeca Afonso, was one of the most beautiful of the festival. Then, from mosh to mini-mosh, Cremalheira do Apocalipse teach us this technique of gentle little jumps, livening up the crowd with this social project from Rio Tinto. Lastly, Seara deserves a mention, for one of the loveliest concerts we saw this edition. It was so beautiful that afterwards we didn’t feel like hearing anything else, staying to absorb the experience and the guitar melodies while stuffing ourselves with pakoras.

III

Better to leave in the dead of night than the next day with the dismantling already under way, we think to ourselves as we drive off towards home, the tedious and tiring job of packing up the tent and rolling everything into little bundles already behind us. We’ll miss this edition, but to close it we listened on the way to the whole of Fausto’s Por Este Rio Acima, one of the best endings we could ever have imagined.

Diluvial Sun - Sunn O))) @ ZDB

by AB

“Gazes unite the peoples in the death of the sun”

Laughing on the plateau of Oechalia, three trembled under three hats: magenta, green and magenta. They had themselves carried by donkey far from the temple, to play down below at jumping off the donkey, at coaxing the beast that never tired of carrying the infinitesimal weight of its debt. They jump off the donkey very hungry and measure the hunger with the donkey, and in the end they climb back on, slowly, upon the weight of the donkey. Off the temple they saw (from the private mythology and in its first fruits, which would bring this story, purifying itself, to the present time) a penance or libation being set up, piercing between the columns, outside, inside, like a rock bird. A man, with one of those hand-cranked cameras, walked alone, walked and listened to the inflections of the soul, and aimed, and the children repeated the kinetic juggling that set the horizontal lines of the temple on fire, and waved, with the delirious breath of the donkey. The Greek man, as was the custom, rolls all the mist into a coat and walks out of the film, abandoning the children who had speculated for him, in besought contemplation, the moving sense of that temple, which would decay in the wind of the centuries, or grow towards each form of the nature of its elements, up to unity, to the sphaeristerium.

It is on the lands of the pelagos that the cloud reappears, swallowing the subjects with their citharas, a cloud that does not transcribe, that acts substantially like a star granting vision on condition that it not be stared at, except through its objects, through the attraction of its weight. Just as the sun is mimicry, the continuous din is mimicry of the sun, the stoning imitates unity inwards like something that wants itself fled and stirs the vital, principles that the ruin that had made it had laid in it. The drum booms continuously through a white phase of tuning, lasting almost the whole scene, and all harmony is veiled when the black silence appears, abandoned by a solemn ecclesiastical cloud (green, magenta, green). The Greek man, in the days before the affliction, considered putting his head out (of the aegis), and listening to the primal rage (of the stone), and searching for the scrawled creatures and the hands that, he had always heard, ran among the carbonic seas of the Peloponnese. He left us in writing: “The lands, and tell your fathers, have already been salted; their thorns have been pulled out; the sun will at last be able to raise them if its order of branching is obeyed; gazes unite the peoples in the death of the sun; hunger gnaws only in the recesses of this death.” I pass on these words on their way to purification, as the children taught me; and may food be born in your mouths.

Southeast Finland seen by LE

Then we got up and headed to the first of many American acts we would see at the festival: Tortoise. I’m writing this short passage sitting on a staircase in the corridors of Amare’s main hall, while in the background the legendary figures of progressive post-rock play a track that sounds as if someone had put on an Ennio Morricone soundtrack with, on top, those sharp reggae guitar upstrokes (also known as skanks or ska strokes, for those in the know). I had decided in advance to write as much as possible with pen and paper, to spend less time fiddling with the computer I carry in my pocket. It would be more organic to walk around with a little notebook, so the creative juices could surface in a more primitive way, I told myself.

It was the second time I had the pleasure of seeing Tortoise live. The first was in Porto, but my 17-year-old self hadn’t been particularly impressed by the jazz sensibilities, an element that has become indispensable to their sound since the album “TNT”. My teenage self, that summer night in 2016, was really only waiting for the artists who played next on the same stage: Drive Like Jehu. A band that, for me, had made that (now nostalgic) night truly complete. Ten years later, and Tortoise were once again a rather tiring band to watch live. One thing is certain about these sensitive, introspective boys who, 30 years after their heyday, still play in a cult band: either they go grey, or they go bald. That said, their second album, Millions Now Living Will Never Die (1996), will always have a special place in my heart. The jazz influences weren’t so marked back then, and during my adolescence I had never heard anything so soothing within the limits of rock. “There’s too much war in this world! I know it sounds corny but it should be said! Let’s get corny!” came from the stage. One of them broke the band’s silence before they started the last piece. The last sentence of that statement in particular stuck in my head and, for the rest of the festival, was often parodied by Bogdanov and me.

“It reminded me of a living-room jam, and we, the audience, just happened to be there, watching.”

The night continued with the second name relevant to Chicago, but no less noteworthy: Jim O’Rourke, a multidisciplinary man who by now has explored practically every corner of the floral musical underworld. That night he was joined by his most recent collaborator, Eiko Ishibashi, a darling of Japanese electroacoustics who, like Jim, has a rather prolific discography. I had gathered that O’Rourke, a native of Illinois, currently lives in Japan, so there must be some connection there. This white-man/Asian-woman duo turned out, without our knowing it at the time, to be the first of many similar artistic partnerships (and perhaps something more?) that we would see that weekend.

Over the years it was above all Jim’s guitar work that left its mark on me; his 1997 record Bad Timing remains, for me, the high point of this eccentric, productive being’s career. The duo’s set consisted of Jim, completely withdrawn into himself, producing bleeps and bloops with a synthesiser and a few ordinary pedals. He did it in a comfortable posture, wearing a cosy winter jumper, a bucket hat and a full grey beard. The Japanese woman produced small dissonant sounds on her flute that she then turned into biting electronic loops. It reminded me of a living-room jam, and we, the audience, just happened to be there, watching. They seemed quite close; I wonder whether Jim speaks Japanese yet. Or is he one of those stubborn “expats” who only want to speak English with other gaijin? Is there something between the two of them after all? I imagine I wasn’t the only one asking.

Oh, and I also thought there was too much stuff on the table: what are you going to do with all that, man? The noise was even a bit rotten, but not in an interesting way. I struggled to stay focused and all I wanted was to go and drink more of the native brew. I came to the conclusion that O’Rourke had reached a unique but more than deserved legendary status, which gave him the freedom to do exactly what he wanted. In other words, big respect, boss.

We left the hall and headed for the big church in the centre of Den Haag. To get to this sacred place we had to cross hordes of people out on a Friday night: city folk filling up on alcoholic drinks and by now far too expensive cigarettes (13 quid a pack in this dump), talking about their busy lives and the highlights of their week. Sometimes it’s easy to become completely alienated from the typical urban being, especially when you surround yourself only with half-broken artistic types who listen only to the most obscure blips instead of yacht DJs like Martin Garrix. Of course there is much to be said about all the art forms that exist between those two extremes, but the contrast was palpable when Anton and I went from the club terraces of Den Haag through the wooden doors of the church, where Drew McDowall (ex-Coil & Psychic TV) replaced the public deep-house noise of the street with something like endless waves of sonic incantations. This was our gospel. This is where we belong.

One of the best contemporary traditions in the drone/ambient world remains this recent acceptance into the church concert circuit, and it’s great that Rewire can take advantage of it too. Pedro Maia’s visuals, by the way, were of a purely transcendent nature and fitted McDowall’s sonic massage perfectly. This is art made for, and by, obsessive and romantic beings.

Drew Mcdowall, Grote Kerk (photo: Charlotte van de Gaag)

The last gig of the night, which everyone was itching for, was Oneohtrix Point Never: Daniel Lopatin, the true master of bleeps and bloops. I think he needs no introduction by now: after a run of much-loved albums like R Plus Seven, Replica, and… who still remembers his pioneering vaporwave alias, Chuck Person? He has also had great success with the soundtracks for the equally “trendy” films of the Safdie brothers. I really like OPN, honestly, but, much to my regret, I couldn’t be there.

That night I was staying with friends in Rotterdam, another of the Randstad’s soulless cities, and apparently there was no public transport connection that would let me see OPN and get to bed the same night. (The risks of being a slave to public transport.) I said a bitter goodbye to Bogdanov after he told me Lopatin is Russian too. Anton had promised me that the next day he would say the concert had been a load of rubbish, really bad. That way I might be able to deal better with my FOMO pain. Hordes of hipsters were filling Amare as I took my bag out of the locker and, eyes on the floor, walked towards the exit looking defeated…

“I remember my mother telling me, before I went to the festival, not to be too harsh in the critique in this text…”

After a good purge, I met Bogdanov again the next day at a decent hour: around 3 p.m. This time we skipped the talks altogether and decided to go and see one of the art installations. Anton is a relatively quiet and introverted Russian, but when it comes to going out, at 30-something he can still last until dawn, and at Rewire he was no exception. He told me the club side had been well done, including the DJ set by Kode9, “the cat man with the third eye”. But, more importantly, he added: “They serve beer at every stage, man. Even in the church there was beer, fuck.”

Bogdanov having a blast in a Hague church

We arrived at a place called “Page Not Found”: outside the festival context, it looked like one of those pseudo-activist urban bookshops, the kind that could exist in any European city, all more or less the same, where you buy poetic coffee-table zines (limited to 150 copies) for the same price as the coffee you drink there. I asked the cute barista where I could find “The Other Family” by Brandon LaBelle: I still hadn’t managed to identify the work that had brought me there. She pointed me outside the bookshop, to the pavement. I replied with an “ohhhhh, of course!”, but I was not in the least prepared for the monstrosity I was about to see. I had sent a photo of the work to my friend Gui, and he swore a schizophrenic homeless person had left his magnum opus there. I remember my mother telling me, before I left for the festival, not to be too harsh in the critique in this text, and not to be overly cynical during the visit (I’m really trying, mum!). So I want at least to thank the artist for the effort he put into this work. Either way, he simply gave himself to his art, and that is by nature a noble gesture. The artist can thank my mother for the contagious #staypositive vibes she always manages to transmit, even when she isn’t there: at that, honestly, she’s really good.

“The Other Family” by Brandon LaBelle

We decided to give a chance to a new name we didn’t know. Our first “bet” of the festival: a band called Apparitions. All we knew beforehand was that there’d be something drone and something metal about it. And with that they had already won us over: we’re both big fans of Sunn O))), but who isn’t, right? “Have you seen the new Earth documentary? Carlson is a real good guy. A really pure dude,” asked Anton. Earth, probably among the first to bet seriously on this kind of sound, along with the Melvins on their manic drone record Lysol. With this bet we hoped to discover new talent within the tradition of extremely slow heavy metal.

The American trio started playing, and it was immediately clear we were witnessing a kind of embrace of the dark arts. A brave meditation unfolded between artist and audience. But something was missing. For me, they could have crushed the audience more with their art of darkness. I would have liked the sound to have more weight than what I could still feel, five minutes earlier, walking among the enormous grey concrete buildings of Den Haag.

After the concert we chatted a while in Portuguese with Igor Imbu, responsible for the electronics on stage: a smart Brazilian who nowadays (also) lives in Chicago (apparently a sort of Mecca for experimental musicians). We thought his parts had been the best of the set, but I didn’t have the courage to admit that his colleague’s drumming, often a bit too stiff, fell short. It was too restrained; the drummer wasn’t really “feeling it”.

Apparitions, Het Spui (photo: Sabine van Nistelrooij)

We were already drinking devilish Duvels again in one of the festival’s many food areas, and that’s where we met Olivier: another visitor who was sleeping in a tent during his three-day crusade. “Yeah, man… in a tent, really… check out this photo, haha. Just in some park… threw my backpack in a hole, buried deep. Yeah, I didn’t have money for a hotel, man… you know how much that costs?” He was a Fleming who had tried to get into the art academy in Ghent no fewer than three times. You really have to love art for that, I thought. We congratulated him on the achievement, but he was already in his third year. “Did you go see weed420, bro? That was fucking amazing, man…” said Olivier. “No, man, unfortunately. But here, have some of this killer weed,” I replied. “Nooo man, I’m not gonna smoke and drink alone, that’s weird.”

We were a bit confused and started discussing it. Why can’t you enjoy a drink or a joint on your own? And how is that stranger than, say, sleeping in a tent in the middle of a metropolitan park like a beggar? We ended up convincing him and even bought him a beer. We drank it harmoniously with two other strangers: a pair of stylish Danish women, also up to their necks in the arts. They sat down with us. They stayed there chilling. We had a long conversation about one of the evangelical religious traumas of one of them’s childhood. Then I said my sister had recently decided to convert to a similar Protestant church and that, in the meantime, she had also got pregnant. “The cycle of trauma continues…” she said with a sad, resigned look. The other really wanted to share that she had Sami blood and that the owner of her label is a policeman. “He puts out our noise records because we have a strong anti-police message, hihi… like ACAB, you know? I think he uses our music as masturbation material.”

These Danish noise sagas only surfaced after a few beers and cigarettes, because at first she seemed rather reserved. I had already come across this kind of BDSM aesthetic in noise, but when she showed me some album covers I was genuinely impressed. The usual gag balls, black leather whips and women being penetrated by two men at once. Fairly graphic content, then… now I understood why she had hesitated so much at first to share the project. (I won her trust by showing her my Iceage T-shirt, a Danish punk band, which I was wearing under my jacket.) “Nobody’s done a digital rip of this yet,” she said. “It only exists physically: this is real underground shit, none of that poser Bandcamp crap.” How can anyone not respect that?

“You really gave the blonde some rizz, man,” Bogdanov said later. He’s already 34, but he still tries to keep up with zoomerspeak vocabulary, without overdoing it though, because that would be cringe. We found the blonde funny, but she wasn’t exactly attractive. I liked the other one better, the brunette, the one with the religious trauma. “You take the gag-ball one, OK?” I suggested, somewhat opportunistically, but Anton refused, the guy’s tough.

Speaking of noise, we went back into the Spui, this time with our new Danish company, and were immediately greeted by Aaron Dilloway’s noisy lines of dialogue. I vaguely knew some of his tapes and had enjoyed his work here and there, but I had no idea what awaited me. The man got to play twice at the festival, and rightly so, as the set was nothing short of masterful. The abstract nature of this kind of “broken art” can often fail miserably; it remains a risky craft. But in the hands of someone who manages to stay connected to their inner voice, without abandoning that meditation during the show, it can result in the most moving sonic expressions. High risk, high reward.

The coherent narrative Dilloway brought to his set was comparable to reading an existential, romantic poem. There was a continuous story, and Aaron successfully took us to different places in his domain. This is what you visit a festival like this for: to see the rawest and noblest sonic experiments succeed. So, hats off to him! This is it.

Aaron Dilloway, Het Spui (photo: Wouter Vellekoop)

“…for many it’s a coordinated dynamic, a healing humiliation ritual, so who cares?”

The night kept a dark note and we carried on with Sumac & Moor Mother. Now, I don’t know either project very well, but Sumac has stuck in my memory since my teens. Back then I had a brief but intense phase of obsession with Aaron Turner (Isis, Old Man Gloom and, recently, Neurosis too), when I had to explore every corner of the genres for “retired” heavy dudes.

Their first record with Keiji Haino is probably the one that impressed me most. The concert started well; Anton and I nodded our heads to the bluesy, dirty grooves the supergroup has been known for since its beginnings. Images came to mind from a fragment of the impressive Old Man Gloom documentary (“Here Is a Gift For You”, from 2014), another Turner-affiliated project (and an equally slow supergroup), in which the gigantic Zozobra, a 15-metre wooden folk effigy, is ritually set on fire in the middle of Santa Fe, New Mexico. In the documentary, the images are accompanied by Aaron and members of bands like Cave-In and Converge giving it everything on an American stage. At the time it was a fresh approach to fusing punk and metal, before it became an obvious mix (back when you could still be properly hated for daring to bring those two worlds together).

Unfortunately I was torn out of this nostalgic groove hypnosis as soon as Moor Mother opened her mouth. Who gave this old woman a stage, man? Am I suddenly at a Brooklyn slam poetry night or what? To make the fever dream even more unbearable, she threw in a casual insult to the whole Dutch people: “Who here is Dutch?”, followed by a quick “I don’t fucking care!” Very subtle, O Moorish mother. You really have to get the nuances to appreciate that joke. Then she bellowed: “Who here still has hope?” We had just 2 seconds to react before she preached again: “That means everyone else doesn’t care!” Well, maybe we do??? I don’t have such quick reflexes, Mother Moor. To add further ambiguity to her speech, she said: “I have hope for MY people. Not for YOUR people.” Ah, good thing you cleared that up, mummy. I was still finding your statements rather open to interpretation and it had caused some confusion. I hope that night you managed to profit handsomely from all your Dutch fans, since, after all, you weren’t willing to share anything of your true self. I understand you want to defend your people, but if you were honest with yourself, you would see that you are simply using these people for your own narcissistic gain. So next time, don’t present it as altruistic and heroic; that’s rather fake and passé. On the other hand, many of those posh white kids who make your shows possible want precisely to be used; for many it’s a coordinated master-and-slave dynamic, a healing humiliation ritual, so who cares? You can have that one, go on.

Mother then carried on with her best Zack de la Rocha impression, while the grey-haired rock and rollers produced feedback in sync, and I… I suddenly felt the urge to apologise right there for all those African slaves my ancestors may have had, man! That’s not on, man!!! But, jokes aside, we’re already about 10 years late for these culture-war issues; we live at the tail end of that controversy. The collective fatigue with this shit set in long ago and, besides, other art critics on Dutch soil have done it much better before me: I mean mainly KIRAC, with their sharp critique of contemporary visual art. (Where’s that Houellebecq film, lads?)

We moved on to Xiu Xiu. Another cult project. But yes, the programme is crammed with this kind of act and, anyway, we had already had to make several sacrifices since getting here. So it wasn’t exactly easy to go hunting for new talent when you had never seen an infamous band like Xiu Xiu live. Though I would have liked to check out more fresh meat. Secretly, what I was hoping for most was the chance to witness one of Jamie Stewart’s fits of rage live: https://packaged-media.redd.it/rvboftlgs50e1/pb/m2-res_480p.mp4?m=DASHPlaylist.mpd&c=wh_ben_en&var=sgpssan&v=1&e=1776823200&s=07a79ad44d2840691ad73afa12874d55bf5d2bf (Big lol).

It was our second concert by a “white man and Asian woman” pair. “You really have to grow your hair and get a Japanese girl to start a noise band,” I told Anton. “Yeah, good idea. I haven’t had much luck with my recent barbers, but now I’ll just let it grow, actually. Not a bad idea at all, man,” he replied, with the air of someone who says blyat every other sentence.

For this show we had got ourselves seats at the top of Amare; we wanted to experience it in a more patrician way. Stewart and Angela were together at a table and looked cooler than ever. Those two are really badass, you know? They still have that rock’n’roll swagger that many of their contemporaries lost ages ago. Angela, on top of that, showed up with a black tie over a white blouse: what a cool woman, man. These guys literally don’t give a shit about anyone, like fuck it, really. Clearly people who still fuck, sex-positive people, you know? That night the esoteric-romantic duo had decided to make an ode to Eraserhead, known as David Lynch’s student cult film. And it is unanimously seen as a classic by those antisocial hermits. Rightly so, actually: Lynch himself always openly flirted with electroacoustic noise of a hypnagogic nature. I was also quite impressed by Xiu Xiu’s reinterpretation of Angelo Badalamenti’s famous soundtrack for Lynch’s Twin Peaks (although, to be honest, I don’t find their more experimental work all that extraordinary).

In short, the concert was without doubt a Lynchian experience. There were few moments of so-called “harsh noise”; those elements were used sparingly and calculatedly. Every now and then Angela brought in a piano melody after the musique concrète passages, just before Stewart blew up balloons and popped them into an SM-58 microphone. But not everything was so refined, because I swear at one point I heard excited moaning, as if from a birth. It felt almost like an ode to the perverse underground, the real scene I mentioned earlier, not to be confused with the conventional poser rubbish you probably listen to.

Anyway, the images on the screen were equally wide-ranging, with an emphasis on Taoist esotericism, ocean foam, aroused BDSM women and, perhaps most interesting, the blank stare of a group of chickens. I immediately remembered that Herzog interview where he describes how disturbing that vacant chicken stare can be. In that moment I felt it to the bone.

Finally, Stewart suddenly sprang up from his chair and smashed 24 beer bottles in a paper bin. He seemed pretty annoyed: good news for me, since I’d been waiting for it. Then he grabbed a broom handle and started stirring all the shards and beer around. The amplified sound of it was delicious! A climax of pure sonic violation for our fragile eardrums: I even got goosebumps, hehe.

The applause that followed was long and well deserved.

Xiu Xiu, Amare (photo: Jan Rijk)

I knew that Saturday night there was still plenty to see in nocturnal Rewire, but unfortunately, as on the previous night, it wasn’t for me.

I met Anton again the following Sunday to see a certain Meetsysteem and his band at Koorenhuis. Our second bet of the festival. But we didn’t last long: the vibe was cute enough, but it sounded as if the guy hadn’t warmed up his vocal cords properly (or maybe he’d spent the previous night at Paard clubbing among Bogdanov and the other ravers?). The music itself sounded a bit like a mix of Spinvis and Bar Italia. There was also something twee-pop about it, something a bit naff. We were half hungover again, so all we wanted was a coffee and a cigarette in the sun, and that’s exactly what we did after 10 minutes of the concert.

“The lyrical themes were surprisingly current and alternated between algorithmic zoomerspeak and archaic teenage angst.”

We were about to go and see Aaron Dilloway a second time, but in the end we took another chance, this time on a group called 33. On the way to the Spui we passed classic esoteric symbols and motifs of the city, above all the Hague stork that appears everywhere as you walk through the old town. Suddenly I thought: wait… 33 is the Freemasons’ number. This can’t be a coincidence: we really have to go and see 33, fuck.

When we got to the Spui, we found a group of Brits on stage with a sound that was a vague mix of everything and anything: a kind of Mr. Bungle but with Björk as the creative brain instead of Mike Patton. An androgynous man of East Asian appearance screamed everything there was to scream, when he wasn’t singing or playing saxophone. Behind him was a buff guy doing backing vocals à la Trent Reznor: he looked like someone who could crowdkill you at a hardcore show, but at the same time like a sales manager about to sell you a vacuum cleaner. The lyrical themes were surprisingly current and alternated between algorithmic zoomerspeak and apocalyptic teenage angst.

Some poetic highlights: “meet me up at the dumpster fire, I lit it up because you let me down. 369… 369… 369…” and, at one point, it spoke of the religious experience of Beatlemania and how a line of speed in the park at sunrise was wonderful and sacred. But the stretches of angelic Bladee-style autotune missed the mark completely. These guys had clearly swallowed the pill of modernity, and weren’t afraid to admit it. We asked the guy at the merch stand whether he had ever studied the numerological implications of the number 33, but the poor thing had no idea and just said he was there helping his friends…

33, Het Spui (photo: Jan Rijk)

In a desperate attempt to escape modernity, I went to Paard looking for The Bug. A confused Lithuanian started talking to us: he too was looking for the big Bug, because there were two Paards and the staff had no idea who The Bug was, or the critter, or whatever. Once we got to Paard 1, the right room, the man enthusiastically started telling us about his Babylonian-experimental reggae band:
“The 12 tribes of Judah are on Mars, man. My thing is intergalactic, you know?” “That guy’s clearly on some heavy shit, isn’t he?” I asked Anton with a grin, as he went to get the second round. But Bogdanov thought he was normal, even too ordinary. I told myself it was just one of those typical Eastern vibes I still can’t decipher, so I let it go.

But before we go any deeper, The Bug took the stage with his case of vinyl and didn’t hesitate to pour 110+ decibel bass grooves into a room full of willing victims. I don’t usually wear earplugs, I’m already half deaf, but those bits of wax really came in handy to protect my hearing from the heaviest post-Jamaican frequencies known to man. This was another form of modernity: different from 33’s. The Bug wasn’t hope, or rage, or love: The Bug was sound itself. It was the whole spectrum. And he filled most of that spectrum, to the brim, with the dissonant harmonies compressed by the bass distortion. No particle of air was spared. The audience was subjugated. The strain of dub the critter has cultivated demands total surrender to the soundsystem. The guy shows no mercy.

Then came Dis Fig: our third “white-man / Asian-woman partnership”. At this festival I had no idea this combination could be so powerful. I’d heard of Yoko Ono and that sort of thing, but I never paid much attention to yellow fever and all that shit. But the doubt always remains: is there tension in these partnerships? Is there a chemistry backstage that we never see on stage?

Dis Fig screamed non-stop: a monolithic punk delivery, something between Atari Teenage Riot and, more recently, the iconic voice of Alice from Crystal Castles. The performance could be described as a shamanic experience: if primordial rituals still exist in our modern secular societies, this was certainly one of them.

The Bug, Paard (photo: Camille Blake)

We had a Chinese crab soup and a few beers to cool our freshly burnt palates. Einstürzende Neubauten, the end of the night and of the festival, awaited us. Or at least the band’s most recent incarnation: a line-up mixing original members with young faces. Blixa Bargeld shone, literally, and masterfully dominated every moment: not a breath was wasted. The proto-industrial rhythms were played with almost military precision and, throughout the concert, were produced with a long and ambitious list of instruments: from cavernous large-scale drills to disposable aluminium parts, toilet pipes, supermarket trolleys and, of course, a series of large steel plates.

Bargeld was truly charismatic and the audience laughed constantly at his jokes, even when mid-concert he introduced the theme of the next song:
“This one is about the national socialist family architecture program.” He wasn’t going to resist, the old German… The audience applauded. “I didn’t know there were nazis in the crowd,” muttered an elderly boomer next to us, wearing one of those classic band-logo T-shirts. “For sure,” said Bogdanov with a grin. The man laughed nervously and his Asian wife left shortly afterwards: “it wasn’t her thing”. Luckily, my company consisted only of a crazy Russian who still found all this funny.

The industrial pioneers played far above expectations, and this wasn’t something we were used to from 80s bands. They even came back for an encore after the audience made some five minutes of desperate racket. During the encore the band sampled sounds from the audience into their loops: intentional or not, either way, brilliant.

Einstürzende Neubauten, Amare (photo: Laura van der Spek)

Einstürzende Neubauten, Amare (photo: Sabine van Nistelrooij)

Tired, and slightly wrecked by all the addictive substances we had consumed to excess over the three days, Anton and I caught a train for a trip together to a good old hole in the province of Gelderland. To escape this noise, after all.

It was fun, Rewire, I got well and truly Rewired, but it also feels good to get back to silence. Far from the noise of the masses of office workers, the metros crossing concrete blocks, the sinister Turkish night shops and the other modern fruits of our hyper-civilised Randstad.

Just one last polite formality (I am my mother’s son, after all): I want to thank the artists, organisers and the rest of the Rewire staff for their excellent and professional work. Practically everything went smoothly and the venues were beautiful.

Rewire, maybe I’ll see you again next year? Thank you!

On 28 March 2026, QV had the chance to witness the play "Filodemo" by Camões, staged by a well-known figure of contemporary Portuguese theatre. It took place in the Sala Estúdio Valentim de Barros, at the Jardins do Bombarda, through the Teatro Nacional D. Maria II.

Filodemo’s Chair - Camões @ Jardins do Bombarda

by CF

(Enter Filodemo) He speaks to us in front of the chairs, still inert for now, setting out a few warnings. Counting up on the way out of the room, I conclude there are ten. Three come with their pair, unlike the rest. One in particular stands out, white, with no back. I would like to sit on one of these chairs, for the simple reason that they are as restless as children. Whenever a child is born, a carpenter is born too, and both will tend the same chair for the rest of their lives. To my displeasure, I only noticed them once already seated. (Exit Filodemo) Yielding his place in vain to other speakers with sixteenth-century names. I am interested only in the chairs and the coordinated movement they acquire. None of the lines spoken can draw me away from this obscure game. Let us return to the children. When they sit down for the first time, they start by kicking. Let us call it the agonising insubordination in the face of life. The chair will stop fitting, and the child finds this idea unbearable. Naturally, it resigns itself to its growth and stays seated in silence. Sometimes it asks if it may leave the table, gets up and goes. During its absence, the carpenter returns. He examines the chair closely, how it has matured. It too has grown alongside the child, each at its own pace. The carpenter wishes to give back to the child that uncontrollable restlessness of the legs, capable of correcting the world. Few carpenters have managed to solve this riddle. Some slowly lose themselves in their own chairs and forget the promise made at birth. This prolonged absence inspires the death of several children and makes the carpenters tremble. Yet, after a few attempts, he seems to have found the solution. He weeps without pause, resting on an incandescent smile. He decides to go home. And it is at that moment that he sees the child.

On 25/11/2025, QV was invited to the magnificent 4th edition of the Sovereign Art Prize in the historical Sociedade Nacional de Belas Artes in Lisboa. The following thoughts ocurred to us during the experience. All Persian references were taken from Saadi´s Gulistan.

The Gallery As A Sacred Rose Garden - Sovereign Art Prize

by GM

The Robbers Of The Kufatcha Tribe - The King Of Nimruz - The Kitten Of Abu Harirah - Selbstüberwindung

All of us gathered to look at icons, worshipping them, drinking the same wine, even eating the same hors d’oeuvres. Not despite its profane entanglements with money and wine and social performance, could a gallery be a holy place? After all, the sacred always requires the mundane as its vehicle.

We, at QV, feel like it’s a convenient time for a slight umdenken of a gallery, that is, reconsidering the concept of a gallery towards a more productive one. For people that are not in a perpetual mind-war with themselves, a gallery can be thought of as a place where wine waters the plants; where paintings are picked like chrysanthemums near the hedge; where we gaze beyond those far-away man-made mountains that in cities go by the name of buildings and are able to enjoy brief moments of contemplation. Cynicism abounds nowadays, an attitude which incidentally is a very effective way of helping the powers to be, and hence the challenge is more how to beautify the concept of a gallery rather than going against it as hard as possible. Revolutions have proven to be just iterations of the previous paradigm. What is truly difficult to attain is a gestalt shift of mentalities, which does not really depend on political conditions, it is more reliant on people’s ability to individuate, something most do not even bother to think about what that even means and neither is it the purpose of this essay to delve into that particular process of the development of our consciousness. We will, instead, endeavour to argue for the gallery in its current form, how perfect it actually is and coming to the simple conclusion that there is really no problem at all with it.

Surprise, money circulates through these spaces. We like to pretend that the marketplace of art stands separate from the marketplace of ideas, of aesthetic experience and of cultural capital and this separation sadly is fantasious. To maintain otherwise is to indulge precisely the cynicism we’ve already renounced. Maybe let’s try and find other ways of existing on this planet besides lackadaisical badmouthing. If we were to put our pink glasses whilst entering the gallery, what we would see before us would be really more like a garden. You plant some new flowers on it, you water them, they grow, you cut them, you sell them or you share them and so on and so on. We would go so far as to specify the plants. Roses, of course. Roses as paintings, as sculptures, figuratively speaking that is. Roses are both beautiful and trigger a visceral reaction of pain, with the thorns and all. Sa’di knew this when he wrote that the rose and the thorn grow from the same root. Really, to want one without the other is to misunderstand beauty, to want the perfume without the hurt. Though there are robbers who would expurgate the gallery of its contradictions, those who want art without commerce, contemplation without the infrastructure of value, without the whole apparatus that keeps the lights on and the wine flowing. These robbers would like to impoverish the space by relieving it of its productive and necessary tensions. What eludes them is that the gallery isn’t diminished by its economic function, only consummated by it.

One who has done many disreputable things,
Cares nothing for the reputation of anyone.

Lest we forget, in the gallery, everyone becomes briefly sovereign. You stand before a painting and it addresses itself to you alone, establishing an I-thou relationship. Everyone has the chance to exercise aesthetic judgment and everyone gets crowned for a moment. Squint and you’ll perceive something utopian here, that of wealth redirected, however temporarily and incompletely, towards the cultivation of beauty and thought; hence the gallery is the best approximation of a contemplative space that a commercial society can produce.

And still, there is room for play, for the gratuitous, for the small, soft and anarchic kitten wandering through Abu Harirah’s grave theological disputations, rubbing against the legs of haughty scholars. Here the kitten is a reminder that not everything needs to be serious and the gallery too must have its small unexpected pleasures or even works that don’t really cohere with curatorial logic. Essentially, we’re talking about moments when the frame disintegrates through charm or some accident or the artist’s refusal to be serious for once.

The gallery’s holiness is not that it’s set apart from the world, though that helps. We’re thinking more along the lines of the fact that it creates occasions for a certain quality of attention the world makes nearly impossible while also giving us the pleasure of maintaining some rituals this very same world has gotten rid off. We would even go so far as to say that what makes it sacred isn’t the fact that’s it’s pure or separated from the rest of the world, it’s more about intensification, like concentrating experience until something breaks through the crust of habit, distraction, of the thousand small deaths we die each day. The white walls and the wine and the hushed voices all serve this intensification, this preparation for the moment when you stop in front of a canvas and something shifts.

After, watch how bodies move through gallery spaces, slowly and deliberately, with a glass providing occupation or excuse, just something to do with the hands, not to mention that the alcohol creates a kind of ceremonial intoxication, a loosening. What we have here is a liminal condition between the quotidian and the aesthetic and we become oh so permeable to experience. Laughingly, meanwhile, the hors d’oeuvres anchor us in our corporeal existence, providing salt on the tongue as the art threatens to dissolve us into pure perception, to make us forget we have bodies at all. While it may seem frivolous, this is nothing but the technology of the sacred, updated for a secular age that has forgotten what the sacred even means and is incapable of admitting it needs it.

What about the Viennese Actionists, you might be wondering. They sought to generate art that resisted commodification, institutionalization, domestication. And where do their works reside now? In galleries, naturally, accruing value that would have appalled their creators, that would have made them weep or rage or both. We could call it a failure. More accurately though, we call it a revelation. The gallery absorbs everything because the gallery is simply the social morphology that aesthetic experience assumes under modernity. To repudiate the gallery is to repudiate sociality itself, to retreat into some prelapsarian fantasy of unmediated experience that never existed in any case. The self-overcoming, the “Selbstüberwindung” we actually need isn’t the overcoming of the gallery, only the overcoming of our petty resentment toward it. Accept that beauty requires architecture, that contemplation requires circumscription, that value requires evaluation. The gallery is so much art’s prison as it is its condition of possibility. The dervish may own nothing and even he needs the caravanserai, the meeting place, the structure that allows him to encounter others, to sit, exchange stories and most importantly, drink tea.

It is undeniable that certain forces have always lit up or dulled the human psyche, with a subsequent effect on the wanderings, leaps, elevations and prostrations of the body. BoCA - Part 1

by GM

Psychogeography of the Gulbenkian - The Art of Obeying the Queue - Flamenco as Modus Educandi - Dialects of Jumpers - The Concert Itself

Walking a thousand times today through these spaces once trodden a thousand times, some god of memory made a point of stroking me with the faces of all the people I knew, and more than knew, with whom time was spent or gained among the leaves and these monoliths, where the act of sitting down was always done somewhat grudgingly, like someone diving into icy water, but soon followed by an inner calm, the kind only obtained through the slight discomforts of daily life. Sweating on the bus or an annoying chill on the arms and other merely physical, slightly unpleasant circumstances that remind us what existing is: not much more than being an animal and having the body process its own ideas, far from the pettiness of the head.

In this way of observing things I found myself when I went to collect my ticket at the newly opened CAM, a more polished version of the old one. You could say it honoured its predecessor, not very notable anyway, at least not as much as the Foundation’s main building or the open-air amphitheatre or the other spaces making up the architectural fabric of the one idyll of the Avenidas Novas. Ticket in hand, I headed for the concert entrance, where there followed that situation so characteristic of modernity and so representative of our tame civilisation. I mean the queue, a.k.a. the “line”. I remember exceptionally well a situation in 2017 when a concert was being prepared in this same place and I climbed a small mound surrounding one section of the amphitheatre to see what was going on. Naturally I was promptly told off by the diligent security guard. The supposed animal instinct is cringe and disrespectful of the norms we have grown used to, so it must be kept well under control. Worse than the guard is hearing someone who feels wronged at having been made1 to wait so long and who, accusing and reciting all the precepts of submission, spits invective into the orbit of the cheeky one who decided to ignore the law. The queue must be respected. Nothing against the event’s organisation. It is more a personal obsession, the kind children have when they come up against the adult world and wonder why things have to be one way and not another. Here the parents answer that it is so because it has always been so. What would the alternative be? Chaos, which for many is the same as saying zero alternatives. Logical conclusion: the queue must be respected.

But there is an alternative: flamenco. It is an art that allows a great deal of improvisation, and it has at its heart a brutal discipline of musical education; only so can one play like that. El duende, which so many expats consider essential to the artistic expression of flamenco, can only be obtained if the body is trained. You don’t have a few drinks in Seville and play a rasgueado straight away. It takes years of gymnastics, and even then true virtuosity reaches few. Like freedom, only a few know how to get it. Guy Debord would be a possible case, but I don’t think his freeloading attitude is worthy of praise. From what I can tell, he was always dependent on others, almost in a kind of permanent adolescence, in a Jungian sense.

And so back to flamenco. Can this show meet the demanding criteria this art form sets itself? Can discipline allow, and indeed be a precondition for, flashes of improvisation? For the moment I was more focused on watching the people. Even before crossing my legs and mentally preparing for the concert about to take place, I put into practice a ritual of considerable anthropological interest in which I strike up conversation with no one and ignore any interaction, however promising. So I noticed the various dialects of jumpers worn by the spectators, which, lit by the stage lights turned towards the audience, gave the impression of a Technicolor mosaic. More than the performance itself, quite often what is most pleasing at a show is seeing who comes for the party, who comes for the art, who can and actually wants to consume culture, and those rare cases embodying all these facets.

The concert began curiously, with field recordings of a workshop while the campaniça players pretended to tune their violas, and this I can tell, because I play too. The fusion that followed between these, with their pairs of steel strings, plus the singer’s incredible voice and the nylon-strung classical guitars, could have been surreal, but was scantly explored. At one point in the show, when everyone played together, I think I actually heard a whole orchestra of experimental music. It was like seeing a kora played alongside a koto, that is, instruments never found together, yet so alike that you would expect someone to have combined them already. Flamenco rarely allows this kind of adventure, and that subversion was perhaps the main merit of this performance of El cante rasgueado, by Niño de Elche and Pedro G. Romero. The rest of the show was conventional: now the flamenco players played, now the campaniça players, sometimes over recordings of cante, which seemed totally atavistic, the timelessness of voice and guitar supplanted by some recordings. Over these, the performer smashed it with his beastly voice, and it was like when a rapper throws ad-libs over the track everyone has already heard at home, when at a concert you would expect something more alive. Personally, I would have put all the guitars together in an orchestra like the one Robert Fripp had in the 80s and told them to improvise at will, with the performer’s voice bursting in whenever he wished. Even so, it was very pleasant to watch excellent musicians doing what they do best, even if there was potential for something far more explosive.

The Biennial should therefore be thanked for encouraging and spreading this kind of exploration, which is exactly what one wants from an association promoting contemporary art. All the more so in a place like the Gulbenkian, so rare. There are many foundations, and gardens even fewer. Its counterpart in, say, London, the Barbican, where even the typeface they use is the same, doesn’t have the same class, and even that city, full of green spaces as it is, has not one garden resembling the Gulbenkian’s, where this concert took place. The whole experience of this show and its surroundings therefore deserved to be crystallised today.
